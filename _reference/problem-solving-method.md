# The five-step method

Use it for every quantitative problem, in every unit.

**Draw → Define → Choose → Solve → Check**

1. **Draw.** Sketch the situation. Pick axes and say which direction is positive. Label what you're given.
2. **Define.** List the knowns and the unknown with symbols, **signs** and units.
3. **Choose.** Name the principle (constant-acceleration kinematics, Newton's second law, conservation of energy, conservation of momentum...) and write the equation **in symbols**.
4. **Solve.** Rearrange for the unknown *first*. Substitute numbers only at the end, with units.
5. **Check.** Are the units right? Is the sign right? Is the size believable? Try an extreme case (what if the mass → 0, the angle → 0°...).

## Example

A car moving at 20 m/s brakes with a constant acceleration of −5 m/s². How far does it travel before stopping?

1. **Draw.** A car moving to the right; call right $+x$.
2. **Define.** $v_{x0} = +20$ m/s, $v_x = 0$, $a_x = -5$ m/s², unknown $\Delta x$. No time is given or wanted.
3. **Choose.** The constant-acceleration equation with no time: $v_x^2 = v_{x0}^2 + 2a_x\,\Delta x$.
4. **Solve.** $\Delta x = \dfrac{v_x^2 - v_{x0}^2}{2a_x} = \dfrac{0 - (20)^2}{2(-5)} = 40$ m.
5. **Check.** The unit is metres ✓. $\Delta x$ is positive: the car moved forward ✓. 20 m/s is 72 km/h, and stopping in 40 m is plausible ✓.

## Why symbols first

- You see *how* the answer depends on each quantity, which is exactly what qualitative questions ask.
- Units cancel visibly, so mistakes show up early.
- One algebra step replaces re-typing a calculator chain when a number changes.
- On the exam, many questions want the answer *in terms of* letters, not numbers.
